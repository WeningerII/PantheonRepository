REVIEWER NOTE
================================================================================

Added the four Diyari figures that published scholarship genuinely individuates beyond the existing core: Marindi, the ancestral dog whose blood became the Parachilna red ochre, the mythic partner of the existing Adnoartina (Horne & Aiston 1924; Howitt 1904; McBryde 1987, 2000); Kutji, the malevolent spirit paired against the existing Mura/Mooramoora from the first ethnography onward (Gason 1874; Howitt 1904; Austin 2013); and the muramura Minkani and Ngura-wordu-punnuna from the Siebert-Howitt legend corpus (Howitt & Siebert, JAI 34, 1904; Howitt 1904; Smith 2019). I verified and excluded near-misses: Paralina (attributed to the Yaurorka, not the Diyari), the Mankara-waka and Mankara-pirna (explicitly two travelling parties of girls, not individuals), the Anti-etya/Ngardu-etya pair as independent entries (Anti-etya is identified with Minkani and the legend is elsewhere credited to Yandruwandha country), and Darana's unnamed youths. Reuther's Killalpaninna manuscript names many further muramura (533 dictionary mentions per Austin), but they lack individuated treatment in accessible published scholarship, so per correctness-over-quantity they were not authored. All four new figures stand alone because the sources record no genealogy, and the one attested tie (Adnoartina slew Marindi) runs from an existing figure to a new one, which the schema's directed kinds cannot express from the new side; it is documented in Marindi's notes and solitary rationale instead.

At his periodic ceremony, held by the Diyari together with neighbouring Lake Eyre peoples such as the Yaurorka, Yantruwunta and Ngameni, the men dig with great care until the 'elbow' of the muramura is uncovered; two men then stand over him and let blood from their opened arm veins fall upon him while the Minkani song is sung. The participants, in a frenzy, strike at one another with their weapons on the mile-long return to camp, until the women rush out and hold shields over their husbands to end the fighting.
