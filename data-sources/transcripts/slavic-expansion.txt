================================================================================
THE GAP
================================================================================

A 48-name check across the WHOLE registry returned 19 hits and 29 absences,
and all nineteen hits were deliberate controls: the registry's own Slavic
pantheon records plus three bogatyrs. Nothing outside the eighteen came back.

That sweep was then re-run, widened to 92 names, after the first nineteen
records had been generated — which is how block VI was found. The second sweep
turned up the worst absence in the tradition and three more attested gods; see
block VI. A sweep is worth re-running once its own results are in the corpus,
because the names that were checked the first time were the names that came to
mind the first time.

Eighteen records is the smallest holding of any tradition in this corpus with
a literature, and the shape of it is unusual. What was there was the East
Slavic pantheon of the Kiev idols — Perun, Veles, Mokosh, Svarog, Dazhbog,
Stribog, Simargl — plus a handful of reconstructed abstractions and three
bogatyrs. What was missing was everything with a physical attestation and
everything with a story:

- **The Rügen temples.** Svetovid of Arkona, whose four-headed statue Saxo
  Grammaticus described and whose temple the Danes destroyed in 1168, was
  absent — and so were Rugievit, Porevit and Porenut, the three multi-headed
  gods of the same island whose statues Saxo also described. These are the
  best-attested Slavic deities there are, because a hostile eyewitness wrote
  down what the statues looked like before knocking them over.
- **The seasonal cycle.** The registry held Marzanna, who is winter and death,
  and neither Jarilo, whom she marries and murders every autumn, nor Zhiva,
  who is born out of her death, nor Kupala, whose midsummer night the cycle
  turns on.
- **The folklore.** No Baba Yaga. No Koschei the Deathless. No Zmey Gorynych.
- **The bylina cast beyond three names.** The registry held Ilya Muromets,
  Dobrynya Nikitich and Alyosha Popovich and not Svyatogor, whom the earth
  cannot carry; not Mikula Selyaninovich, the ploughman who is stronger than
  all of them; not Sadko; not the Nightingale the Robber; and not Prince
  Vladimir, whose court the whole Kiev cycle is set at.
- **The bird-maidens.** Alkonost, Sirin and Gamayun.
- **One of the six idols of Kiev.** The Primary Chronicle's entry for 980 lists
  the gods Vladimir set up on the hill: Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl
  and Mokosh. The registry held five of the six. It did not hold **Khors**, who
  is the most frequently mentioned Slavic god after Perun. This was found by the
  second sweep, not the first.
- **Three more chronicle-attested West Slavic gods.** Yarovit, whose temple at
  Wolgast held a shield instead of a statue; and Prove and Podaga, the two
  Wagrian gods Helmold names, one worshipped at an oak grove with no image and
  one at an image with no grove.

================================================================================
WHAT THIS BATCH DOES NOT INGEST
================================================================================

**The household and landscape spirits are classes.** Domovoi, rusalka, leshy,
vodyanoy, bannik, kikimora and vila are kinds of being — there is a domovoi
behind every stove in the Slavic world, not one Domovoi — and this programme
does not record kinds. They are REJECT rows.

**The bird-maidens are not.** Alkonost, Sirin and Gamayun are three named
individuals with distinct offices, depicted as three in the same lubok prints,
and they go in as three records.

================================================================================
SOURCING NOTE
================================================================================

No direct outbound web access in this session — fetches are refused at the
proxy — so research went through server-side web search, and the citations
name the reference treatments consulted. Slavic mythology has a particular
source problem, which the records state where it bites: there is almost no
native written tradition, the best descriptions of the gods are by hostile
Christian chroniclers, and a good deal of what circulates as Slavic mythology
is nineteenth-century Romantic reconstruction. Where a figure rests on
reconstruction rather than attestation the record says so.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — II. THE SEASONAL CYCLE
================================================================================

The registry held Marzanna, who is winter and death, and held nothing she acts
on. The reconstructed Slavic year is a cycle of four: a god who is born, grows,
marries, betrays and is killed; the goddess who kills him and then dies herself;
the life that comes out of her death; and the midsummer night on which the turn
happens. Three of those four were missing.

These four records are the most heavily qualified in this batch, and the
qualification is on the records, not buried here. Jarilo's myth is a twentieth-
century reconstruction by Radoslav Katičić and Vitomir Belaj out of comparative
folk-song material; Kupala as a *god* is a personification of a festival, named
as an idol only in a seventeenth-century chronicle; Devana rests on Jan Długosz,
whose Slavic pantheon most modern scholars do not accept. Živa alone has a
straightforward medieval attestation, in Helmold's Chronica Slavorum. Each
record says which of these it is.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — III. THE THREE ANTAGONISTS OF THE FOLKTALE
================================================================================

These three are the best-known Slavic figures in the world and the registry held
none of them. They are not deities and they are not recorded as deities; they are
the antagonists of the East Slavic wonder-tale and the bylina, and they are named
individuals rather than classes — there is one Koschei and one Baba Yaga, which
is exactly what distinguishes them from the domovoi and the rusalka that this
batch rejects.

Zmey Gorynych is the reason the omission mattered most: the registry already held
Dobrynya Nikitich, and Dobrynya's single defining deed is killing this dragon.

================================================================================

The registry held Ilya Muromets, Dobrynya Nikitich and Alyosha Popovich, the
three bogatyrs of the standard triad, connected to each other by companion edges
and to nothing else. It held no court for them to serve at, no opponent for any
of them to fight, and neither of the two figures the epic tradition itself
treats as stronger than all three.

Five records: the giant the earth will not carry; the ploughman who is stronger
than the giant; the Novgorod guslar, who belongs to the other cycle entirely;
the robber Ilya brings in on a stirrup; and the prince whose hall the whole
Kievan cycle is set in.

AUTHORED FIGURES — V. THE BIRD-MAIDENS
================================================================================

Three named bird-women, grouped as a triad in the Slavic tradition itself and
printed together on the same lubok sheets: joy, sorrow and prophecy. This is
the distinction the batch draws between an individual and a class — there is one
Gamayun, and there is a rusalka in every river.

The companion edges among the three are reciprocated inside the batch and their
notes say exactly what they rest on, which is a pictorial and typological
grouping rather than a narrative in which the three meet.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — VI. FOUR GODS THE SWEEP TURNED UP AFTER THE FACT
================================================================================

These four were found by re-running the corpus sweep after the first nineteen
records were generated, which is why they are in a sixth block rather than the
first. One of them is the most consequential single absence in the tradition.

**The registry held five of the six idols of Kiev.** The Primary Chronicle, under
the year 980, records that Vladimir set up idols on the hill outside his hall:
Perun with a head of silver and a moustache of gold, and Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog,
Simargl and Mokosh. The registry held Perun, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl and
Mokosh. It did not hold Khors — who is, after Perun, the most frequently
mentioned god in the entire East Slavic written record.

The other three are West Slavic gods named by the two chroniclers this tradition
depends on: Yarovit from the Lives of Otto of Bamberg, and Prove and Podaga from
Helmold, who names Prove and Živa in the same sentence.
