PANTHEON REGISTRY — FIGURE-AUTHORING TRANSCRIPT
Traditions: Canaanite (Ugaritic) and Phoenician
Eras (registered, exact):
  Canaanite  — cosmogonic | mythic | heroic-age
  Phoenician — mythic | phoenician-archaic | phoenician-classical |
               phoenician-colonial | punic
Author pass: Canaanite held 19 records and Phoenician 7. The Ugaritic tablets
             are among the best-preserved bodies of Bronze Age myth in
             existence — the Baal Cycle, the Kirta epic and the Aqhat epic are
             continuous narratives with named casts — and the registry was
             carrying the principals without the casts.

================================================================================
THE GAP
================================================================================

A corpus-wide name check, over canonical names and aliases with Unicode
normalisation and across the WHOLE registry, ran 56 candidates. Five resolved
to existing records. **Fifty-one were absent**, among them:

- **Athtar**, who climbs onto Baal's empty throne and whose feet do not reach
  the footstool — the most quoted scene in the Baal Cycle after the fight with
  Yam, and the closest Near Eastern parallel to Isaiah's fallen morning star.
  (The South Arabian cognate Athtar is in the registry under Sabaean and
  Aksumite; the Ugaritic god is not.)
- **Pidray, Tallay and Arsay** — Baal's three daughters.
- **Gupan and Ugar**, his two messengers, and **Qudshu-wa-Amrur**, Athirat's.
- **Lotan**, the seven-headed serpent, and **Tunnanu**, the dragon Anat strikes
  down. The registry carried Leviathan under Yahwism and not the Ugaritic
  serpent the Hebrew text is quoting.
- **Shahar and Shalim**, Dawn and Dusk, the subject of their own tablet.
- **Horon**, **Milku**, **Rapiu** and **Ilib** — the exorcist, the underworld
  king, the singular of Rephaim, and the ancestral god.
- **The entire human cast of the Kirta epic**: Pabil, Hurraya, Yassib, Ilhu,
  Thitmanat and Shataqat, the healer El makes out of clay when no god will
  answer him. The registry held Kirta alone.
- **Yatpan**, who kills Aqhat, in a registry holding Aqhat and Paghat.
- **Baalat Gebal**, the chief deity of Byblos, and most of the cast of Philo of
  Byblos's *Phoenician History*.

================================================================================
SOURCING NOTE
================================================================================

No direct outbound web access in this session — every fetch is refused at the
proxy — so research went through server-side web search and the citations name
what was actually consulted: the standard reference treatments of the Ugaritic
deities, and the primary texts where the claim comes from the text itself (the
Baal Cycle, KTU 1.23 "Dawn and Dusk", KTU 1.100, the Kirta epic, the Aqhat
epic, and Philo of Byblos's *Phoenician History* as preserved by Eusebius). No
printed monograph is cited as though it had been read. Where the reference
literature records a scholarly disagreement — whether Arsay belongs with the
other two daughters, whether Milku and Rapiu are one god — the record states
the disagreement instead of settling it.

================================================================================
TWO PRE-EXISTING RECORDS THIS PASS MODIFIES, AND WHY
================================================================================

`enemy` is a symmetric relation kind, and the two defining facts about Lotan
and Tunnanu are who kills them. Recording those on the new records alone would
leave the corpus one-legged, so `canaanite_baal` gains `enemy → canaanite_lotan`
and `canaanite_anat` gains `enemy → canaanite_tunnanu`, both added to their own
transcript. Nothing else about either record changes, and the record-level diff
against origin/main is checked to prove it.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — IV. PHOENICIAN: BYBLOS, AND PHILO'S COSMOGONY
================================================================================

The Phoenician tradition held seven records — Astarte, Baal Hammon,
Baalshamin, Eshmun, Melqart, Resheph, Tanit — and not the chief deity of
Byblos, nor any of the cast of the one surviving Phoenician cosmogony.

That cosmogony comes through Philo of Byblos, who claims to be translating a
much older Phoenician writer, Sanchuniathon, and survives only because
Eusebius quoted him at length in order to attack him. Philo is euhemeristic:
he presents gods as ancient kings and inventors. The Ras Shamra tablets,
found in 1929, turned out to corroborate a good deal of what he reports, which
is why the material is taken seriously now and was not in 1850. These records
say on their face that the source is Philo, and what Philo is.
