Tradition: Yoruba

  oduduwa-foundation | ife-classical | oyo-empire | post-oyo | colonial
Author pass: Yoruba held 20 records for a tradition whose own reckoning counts
             its divinities in the hundreds — the commonly cited figure is four
             hundred and one, and one reference treatment puts the pantheon as
             high as six thousand. This adds 32, taking Yoruba from 20 to 52.

================================================================================
THE GAP, AND WHAT THIS BATCH DOES AND DOES NOT CLOSE
================================================================================

A 72-name check across the WHOLE registry returned 24 hits and 48 absences.
Three of the 24 hits are false positives on the normalised name — `idoma_aje`
is an Idoma figure and not Aje the orisha of wealth, `tahitian_oro` is not the
Yoruba Oro, and `santeria_elegua` is the Cuban form of Eshu, who was already
here under his own name. The absences include several that are structurally
odd:

- **Oshosi**, the hunter orisha, was in the registry — as `santeria_ochosi`,
  under Cuban Santería. The diaspora form was present and the Yoruba original
  was not. (The same pattern this programme found with Nanaya and the Sogdian
  Nana, and with Lotan and the Hebrew Leviathan.)
- **Oba**, the senior wife of Shango, in a registry holding Shango, Oya and
  Oshun — three of the four principals of that household and not the fourth.
- **Yemowo**, the consort of Obatala, in a registry holding Obatala.
- **Ori** — the personal head, the destiny each person kneels and chooses
  before Olodumare — which is arguably the most-invoked religious object in
  Yoruba practice and which the registry did not have.
- **Iku**, death, who heads the eight chief Ajogun.
- **Moremi Ajasoro**, the heroine of Ife, in a registry holding her husband
  Oranmiyan.
- **The Oyo ruling line beyond Shango, Aganju and Ajaka** — which is to say
  the whole of it with dates on. No Orompoto, the first female Alaafin; no
  Ojigi, who brought Dahomey to tribute in 1730; none of the eight
  eighteenth-century Alaafins destroyed by their own council; no Bashorun Gaa,
  who destroyed four of them; no Abiodun, no Aole, no Afonja, no Oluewu, no
  Atiba. The registry held three Oyo rulers and stopped.

**This batch is still smaller than the gap, and deliberately so.** Yoruba
religion is very well documented in some places and very thinly documented in
the sources reachable from this session in others, and several strong
candidates — Logunede, Obalufon, Odu, Oke, Ayao, Olosa, Onile, Aroni, and
Egungun as a named figure rather than as an institution — could not be
substantiated here at all. They are recorded BLOCKED with the exact
uncertainty rather than written from memory. Thirty-two verified additions
that take the tradition from 20 records to 52 are worth more than sixty of
which a third are guesses; the census says which is which.

================================================================================
SOURCING NOTE
================================================================================

No direct outbound web access in this session — every fetch is refused at the
proxy — so research went through server-side web search and the citations name
what was consulted: the standard reference treatments of the individual orisha
and of the Oyo and Ife rulers. No printed monograph is cited as though it had
been read, and no Ifa verse is cited that was not returned by the search.

One caution particular to this tradition: a great deal of what circulates
online about the orisha is Santería or Candomblé practice presented as Yoruba
practice, and the two have diverged over four centuries. Where a record rests
on diaspora material it says so; where the Yoruba and the diaspora forms are
both in this registry they are linked and kept separate.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — II. IFE AND OYO: THE PEOPLE
================================================================================

The registry held Oranmiyan and not his wife; it held Shango, Aganju and Ajaka
and then nothing for the next three hundred years of the Oyo line, which is the
part with dates on it. What follows is ten people: two from the Ife foundation
cycle and eight from the Oyo empire and its collapse.

Two cautions, both recorded in the records themselves rather than smoothed out.

First, Oyo regnal chronology before the eighteenth century is oral and
contested. Ajagbo is credited with a hundred and forty years on the throne,
which no one believes literally; scholars hold that the reigns of other
Alaafins have been absorbed into his. The record says so.

Second, the sources reachable here disagree about when Afonja died — one gives
c. 1813, others put his killing in 1823 or early 1824, after the 1817 secession
of Ilorin and after Alimi's death in 1823. The record carries the disagreement
instead of choosing.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — III. THE PARROT'S EGG: EIGHT EIGHTEENTH-CENTURY ALAAFINS
================================================================================

Oyo's constitution gave the Oyo Mesi, the council of seven under the Bashorun,
the power to reject an Alaafin: they sent him a covered calabash or a parrot's
egg, and he was then obliged to take his own life. The eighteenth century is
the record of what happens when that instrument is used routinely, and it ends
with an empire that cannot govern itself. Eight of the men on the receiving end
are here. None of the eight was in the registry.

Two internal contradictions in the reachable sources are recorded rather than
resolved. Gberu's reign is given as c. 1735-1746 and his successor
Amuniwaiye's as c. 1738-1742, which cannot both be right; and Onisile's
accession is given as 1746 while the Dahomey campaigns attributed to his reign
are dated 1742 and 1743. Both records say so on their face.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — IV. IBADAN AND THE NOMINAL CROWN
================================================================================

The settlement that ended the Oyo collapse had two parties. Atiba had the title
and Ibadan had the army, and the man who agreed to hold the army under the
title was Abiodun's grandson. He is here for that reason and no other; the rest
of Ibadan's nineteenth-century leadership — Oluyedun, Oderinlo, Ogunmola,
Ibikunle, Latoosa — is real, is absent from the registry, and belongs to an
Ibadan pass rather than to this one. The census says so under BLOCKED.
