================================================================================
THE GAP: A TRADITION WITH NO HISTORY IN IT
================================================================================

A 79-name check across the WHOLE registry returned 9 hits and 70 absences, and
several of the 9 are alias matches rather than real holdings — "Kisin" resolves
to `maya_ah_puch` through an alias, "Ah Mun" to `maya_yum_kaax`, "Chac Chel" to
`maya_ix_chel`, "God D" to `maya_itzamna`, "Maize God" to three different
records at once.

The gap has two shapes and they are not the same problem.

**The Popol Vuh was in the registry as a cast list with the extras cut.** The
corpus held Hun Hunahpu, the Hero Twins, Xquic, Xmucane, Xpiacoc, Hun-Came,
Vucub-Came, Tohil, Vucub Caquix, Zipacna, Cabracan, Camazotz and Cuchumaquic —
and not: Hun Batz and Hun Chouen, the elder half-brothers turned into monkeys,
who are the patrons of every Maya scribe and artist; their mother Xbaquiyalo;
nine of the twelve lords of Xibalba; Avilix and Hacavitz, without whom Tohil is
one god rather than three; the four first men made of maize and their four
wives, who are the whole point of the book; the four destroyers of the wooden
people; and Xulu and Pacam, the two seers whose advice is the mechanism by
which the Hero Twins come back from the dead.

**The Classic period was absent entirely.** Not a single Maya ruler was in the
registry — no Pakal, no Yax K'uk' Mo', no Jasaw Chan K'awiil, no Yuknoom the
Great — in a corpus that carries Egyptian pharaohs, Mesopotamian kings, the
whole Mexica tlatoani line as of last month, and ten Mixtec lords by their
calendar names. Nor were the gods that Classic inscriptions and ceramics
actually name: K'awiil, God L, Pauahtun, the four Bacabs, GI and GIII of the
Palenque Triad, Xaman Ek, Buluc Chabtan.

The Maya are the one Mesoamerican civilisation that wrote its own political
history down, in a script that has been readable since the 1980s, and the
registry had the mythology and none of the history.

================================================================================
THE TRADITION-KEY PROBLEM, OBSERVED AND NOT TOUCHED
================================================================================

The corpus carries the Maya across five keys — Maya (26), K'iche' (12),
Yucatec Maya (12), Lacandon (12), Tzotzil–Tzeltal (9) — and the first three
overlap heavily:

- **All twelve K'iche' records duplicate Maya records.** kiche_hun_came /
  maya_hun_came, kiche_tohil / maya_tohil, kiche_xpiyacoc / maya_xpiacoc, and
  so on through the whole Popol Vuh set.
- **Seven of the twelve Yucatec Maya records duplicate Maya records**: Ah
  Puch, Chaac, Itzamna, Ix Chel, Kinich Ahau, Kukulkan, Yum Kaax.

That is nineteen figures held twice or three times under different keys. It is
pre-existing, it is flagged in the census, and **it is not merged here**:
merging is destructive and the tradition-key question is the owner's.

This batch goes under **Maya**, because that is the key with the periodised
eras (cosmogonic → colonial) that Classic-period material needs, and because
it is the key the fuller Popol Vuh set already sits under. Every new record
that pairs with an existing one points at the `maya_*` id.

================================================================================
SOURCING NOTE
================================================================================

No direct outbound web access in this session — fetches are refused at the
proxy — so research went through server-side web search and every citation
names what was consulted. Epigraphic dates are given as the reference
treatments give them, in the Gregorian conversions those treatments use, and
where two treatments disagree the record says so rather than averaging.

One caution particular to this tradition: Classic Maya theonyms are still
partly read by letter-labels (God D, God L, God N, GI, GII, GIII) assigned by
Schellhas and by the Palenque epigraphers before the names could be read.
Where a label has since been resolved to a name it is used and the label kept
as an alias; where it has not, the label is the record's name and the record
says why.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — II. THE GODS THE CLASSIC INSCRIPTIONS ACTUALLY NAME
================================================================================

The registry's Maya gods are the ones the sixteenth-century Spanish sources
list — Itzamna, Chaac, Ix Chel, Kinich Ahau, Yum Kaax, Ah Puch, Kukulkan.
Those are real and they are late. The gods that appear on Classic monuments
and painted vessels, in the script that has been readable for forty years,
were absent: K'awiil, who is in the name of half the kings in this batch; God
L, who is on more Late Classic vases than anyone; Pauahtun and his four
Bacabs, who hold the sky up.

Two conventions, stated once. Where a letter-label (God D, God L, God N, GI,
GII, GIII) has since been resolved to a readable name, the name is the
record's primary and the label is an alias. Where it has not been resolved —
God L is the standing case — the label is the primary, because inventing a
name would be worse.

Two candidates that look like additions are deliberately NOT here. The
**Tonsured Maize God** of Classic art is identified by the reference
treatments with Hun Hunahpu, who is already in the corpus, and a second record
would be the duplicate this programme forbids. **Chac Chel** is already
carried as an alias on `maya_ix_chel`. Both are census rows, not records.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — III. THE CLASSIC PERIOD: TWENTY-THREE PEOPLE WITH DATES
================================================================================

Before this batch the registry held zero Maya rulers. Not few — zero. The
Classic Maya are the only Mesoamerican civilisation that recorded its own
dynastic history in a phonetic script, in day-exact dates, on monuments that
are still standing, and the corpus carried none of it while carrying Egyptian
pharaohs, Mesopotamian kings, the Mixtec lords and, as of this programme's
last batch, the entire Mexica tlatoani line.

What follows is twenty-three of them, from six cities, chosen so that the
political history is legible rather than sampled: Tikal and Calakmul, who
fought each other for two centuries; Palenque, whose kings wrote theology to
justify an irregular succession; Copán and Quiriguá, where a client beheaded
his overlord; Yaxchilán, where a king's wife is on more monuments than most
kings; and El Perú-Waka', where the woman outranked her husband and the
inscriptions say so.

Dates are Gregorian conversions as the reference treatments give them. Where
two treatments differ the record says so.
