================================================================================
REVIEWER NOTE
================================================================================

Added the two named culture heroes of Zegwaard's Asmat headhunting charter myth: Desoipitsj ("man with a wound"), the elder brother whose severed, speaking head instituted headhunting and male initiation, and Biwiripitsj/Beworpitsj ("parrot man"), the younger brother who performed the first beheading; both are attested by name with etymologies in Zegwaard (American Anthropologist, 1959) and discussed in Pouwer (KITLV, 2010), de Hontheim (Anthropological Forum, 2010) and Hoffman (2014). Beworpits, flagged in the task notes, proved to be the same parrot-man figure (de Hontheim's spelling Beworpitsj), so it is recorded as an alternate name rather than a separate entry. No further individuated figures survived verification: "Teweraut" is a modern novel/festival name without scholarly mythological attestation, "Tumun" appears only on non-scholarly websites, and the jipae mask and bisj-pole literatures name only recently deceased persons, while Konrad & Konrad (1996) and the museum literature yield no additional citable named beings beyond Fumeripits and these brothers.

His elder brother Desoipitsj then demanded that the same be done to himself, and Biwiripitsj, after resisting, speared him, severed his head with a bamboo knife, and thereafter obediently executed every order the still-speaking head gave: the butchering of the body, the preparation of the decorated trophy skull, and the first initiation of a boy, in which the head is submerged in water and carried at a run to the bachelors' house, and the novice assumes the name of the slain man. He is thus the first headhunter, and the Asmat warrior who takes his victim's name repeats what Biwiripitsj first did. Under the spelling Beworpitsj the figure remained, into the twenty-first century, a model of behaviour celebrated in myths, songs, rituals and objects connected with the former headhunting complex, and the name is preserved in modern Asmat cultural festivals at Agats."
