Beneath Molu stands a populous order of unnamed nature-spirits, collectively the ab-spirits, which inhabit particular trees, springs, wells and stretches of wild country and which are addressed with small offerings and protective charms. The most consequential public cult was directed at rain: at the opening of the agricultural season priests and elders performed ceremonies at sacred trees and stones, calling on Molu and on the ancestral dead to release the seasonal downpours over the mountains. Centuries of Islamization have not erased this frame so much as recoded it; the name of Allah is now spoken where Molu once was, the ancestral guardians of springs have become the saints of local shrines, and rain-rites persist as agricultural festivals. Sources differ on how far the older theology survived independent of Islam by the time it was first written down, but the figure of a single overarching god above a graded world of place-bound spirits is consistently reported."

In the standard telling a wandering Arab of noble descent, lamed in the leg or heel, arrives from the north as a penniless stranger at the court of a Fur chief; his wisdom in settling disputes wins him the chief's favour and, in one widespread version, the chief's own repudiated wife, a daughter of the ruling house. From this union the royal line descends. Traditions assign him sons who become eponyms of peoples and dynasties: Ahmad Kunjar, ancestor of the Kunjara section of the Fur from whom the Keira sprang, and Musa, who carried the line west to found rule in Wadai. His epithet al-Maqur, 'the hamstrung', is itself woven into the tale as the mark of an old wound. Scholars read the legend as a charter that grafts an Arab and Islamic pedigree onto an older indigenous kingship; the several oral versions disagree over which chief received him and which woman bore his heirs, and the same charter is claimed, with variation, by neighbouring dynasties."
