================================================================================
THE GAP: 72 CHECKED, 70 ABSENT
================================================================================

A 72-name check across the WHOLE registry returned two hits, and both are
false. "Yanluo Wang" matched `buddhist_yama` and `hindu_yama` — related
figures in a transmission chain, not the Chinese king of Diyu. "Tudigong"
matched `baule_asie`, an unrelated West African earth deity, on a normalised
alias.

Seventy absences out of seventy-two, in the religious corpus with more
adherents and more surviving text than any other in this registry. What was
missing was not a periphery. It was:

- **The Three Pure Ones** — Yuanshi Tianzun, Lingbao Tianzun and Daode
  Tianzun, who are the highest deities in Daoism, all three absent, in a
  registry that held the Jade Emperor (who is below them).
- **Six of the Eight Immortals.** The registry held Lü Dongbin and He Xiangu
  and not the other six, which is like holding two Olympians.
- **The entire underworld.** No Yanluo Wang. No Meng Po, whose soup of
  forgetting every Chinese soul drinks before rebirth. No Zhong Kui, no Ox-Head
  and Horse-Face, no Black and White Impermanence.
- **The whole cast of the two novels the popular pantheon actually comes
  from.** The registry held Sun Wukong and not Tang Sanzang, Zhu Bajie or Sha
  Wujing — the Monkey King without the pilgrimage. It held Li Jing, Nezha,
  Jinzha and Muzha from the *Fengshen Yanyi* and not Jiang Ziya, who is the
  book's protagonist, nor Daji, nor King Zhou, nor King Wen, nor King Wu.
- **Chiyou, Xingtian, Kuafu and Jingwei** — the four best-known figures in the
  whole of early Chinese myth after the sovereigns.
- **The Four Perils.**
- **Guan Yu and Mazu**, who between them have more temples than any other two
  figures in the Chinese world.

================================================================================
TWO SCOPING DECISIONS, STATED UP FRONT
================================================================================

**Offices are not figures.** Chenghuang, the City God, and Tudigong, the Earth
God, are divine offices held by a different deified local person in every town
and every field in China. The reference treatments define them that way — "a
City God is a tutelary deity ... of a particular village, town or city" — which
makes them a class, and this programme does not ingest classes. They are
REJECT rows with the reasoning, not records, on the same rule that turned away
the Ajogun, the Bacabs-as-a-class and the Are Ona Kakanfo. If the owner wants
office-deities as records, these two are the first ADDs.

**The imperial line is deferred as a unit.** Qin Shi Huang, Wu Zetian, Han
Wudi and two thousand years of emperors are real, absent, and not here:
sampling two of several hundred would be exactly the arbitrary partial the
census exists to prevent. What is here from the historical record is the
*deified*: Guan Yu, Mazu and Zhang Daoling, who receive cult.

SOURCING NOTE
================================================================================

No direct outbound web access in this session — fetches are refused at the
proxy — so research went through server-side web search and every citation
names what was consulted. Where a figure comes from a datable literary source
— the *Xiyouji* of the sixteenth century, the *Fengshen Yanyi* of the same
period, the *Shanhaijing* of the Warring States and Han — the record says so
rather than presenting a novel's invention as ancient myth.

A note on eras. The Eight Immortals are traditionally dated to specific
dynasties — Zhongli Quan to the Han, Zhang Guolao and Han Xiangzi to the Tang,
Cao Guojiu to the Song — but the two already in this registry are filed under
`mythic`, and these six are filed the same way so the group does not split
across six era buckets. The traditional dating is on each record.

AUTHORED FIGURES — II. SIX OF THE EIGHT IMMORTALS
================================================================================

The registry held Lü Dongbin and He Xiangu. The Eight Immortals are a group of
eight by definition — they are named, painted, carved and invoked as a set,
and "the Eight Immortals cross the sea" is one of the two or three best-known
scenes in Chinese art. Holding two of them is not holding a fraction of a
pantheon; it is holding a fragment of one object.

Each of the six is given the attribute that identifies him in every
representation, because in this group the object is the identification: the
fan, the donkey, the flute, the court tablet, the iron crutch, the basket of
flowers.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — III. DIYU: THE UNDERWORLD AS A CIVIL SERVICE
================================================================================

The Chinese afterlife is administered. A soul is arrested by two officers,
brought before a court, processed through nine more, and finally given a drink
that clears its memory so it can be filed back into the world. Every stage has
a named holder, and the registry had none of them — no Yanluo Wang, no Meng
Po, no Zhong Kui, no Ox-Head and Horse-Face, no Black and White Impermanence.

Diyu itself is a Buddhist import reworked by Daoism and by folk practice,
which is why Yanluo Wang's name is the Chinese form of Yama. The corpus holds
`buddhist_yama` and `hindu_yama`; this record is the Chinese magistrate at the
end of that transmission, which is a different office in a different system,
and the relation between them is stated rather than the records merged.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — IV. THE INVESTITURE OF THE GODS
================================================================================

The *Fengshen Yanyi*, a hundred-chapter novel of the Ming, first printed
between 1567 and 1619, is where a large part of the Chinese popular pantheon
comes from: it tells the fall of the Shang and the rise of the Zhou as a war
in which the casualties are enfeoffed as gods afterwards, off a list. The
registry held four of its supernatural cast — Li Jing, Nezha, Jinzha, Muzha —
and none of its principals.

These records say on their face that they come from a sixteenth-century novel
working on an eleventh-century-BC event, because the difference matters and
the genre does not hide it.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — VI. THE ANCIENT MYTH: MONSTERS, GIANTS AND THE FOUR PERILS
================================================================================

The oldest layer, most of it from the *Shanhaijing* and the *Zuo Zhuan*. The
registry held the sovereigns — Fuxi, Nüwa, Shennong, Huangdi, Yao, Shun, Yu —
and not the figures they are defined against: no Chiyou, whom Huangdi fought
for ten years; no Xingtian, who kept fighting after decapitation; no Kuafu,
who chased the sun; no Jingwei, the drowned girl who is trying to fill in the
sea. These are the four most-quoted figures in early Chinese myth after the
sovereigns themselves, and they were absent from a registry that held the
sovereigns.

Also here: the Four Perils, and Xihe, who is the mother of the ten suns that
the registry's own Houyi shot down.

================================================================================
AUTHORED FIGURES — VII. CULTURE HEROES, AND THREE PEOPLE WHO BECAME GODS
================================================================================

Two groups that close the batch. First the culture heroes at the front of the
Chinese sequence whom the registry skipped — the Four Clans are Youchao,
Suiren, Fuxi and Shennong, and the corpus held the last two — plus Cangjie,
who invented writing. Then the three figures who were people and are now gods:
Zhang Daoling, who founded organised Daoism; Guan Yu, a general of the third
century who is now the god of loyalty; and Mazu, a girl from a Fujian island
who is now the Queen of Heaven.
