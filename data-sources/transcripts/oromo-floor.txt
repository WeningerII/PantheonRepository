REVIEWER NOTE
================================================================================

Added five individuated figures the recently authored core lacked: Lafa, the Earth, Waaqa's female counterpart and 'wife' (Bartels 1983; Ta'a, EJOSSAH 2012; Kelbessa 2022); Horoo, the apical ancestor created from the waters of Walaabuu (Hinew, STAR Journal 2012; Kelbessa 2022); Akkoo Manooyyee, the legendary queen of the era of women's rule still invoked in Guji rain prayers (Imana, EAJSSH 2023; IJIRD 2018); Makkoo Bilii, the Macha lawgiver-prophet of Odaa Bisil (Triulzi, Paideuma 1990; Mohammed Hassen 1990); and Holawaka, Waaqa's messenger bird in the origin-of-death myth (Frazer 1918; Scheub 2000). Deliberately excluded after source checks: the first woman Hortu, named only through a single chain (Megerssa apud Kelbessa 2022) and therefore folded into Horoo's entry; the moiety eponyms Borana and Barentu, genealogical charter figures attested since Bahrey (1593) but not religiously individuated; and the alleged Arsi divinity 'Boorantichaa', for which no verifiable scholarly attestation could be found. Waaqeffanna's strongly monotheistic structure means the remaining spirit vocabulary (ekeraa, jaarii, clan ayyaana) is class-level rather than individuated, so this set now approaches the attested limit of named figures.

The Guji claim her especially, but many Oromo communities tell of her as their one-time sovereign: a formidable and finally tyrannical ruler under whom men performed the housework and were set labours of studied impossibility, until, weary of her orders, they conspired and brought her down. Scholars read the tale as a charter narrative reflecting a remembered or imagined matriarchal past and its replacement by the male-centred gadaa polity, and as a key text in Oromo debates about the standing of women.
